From Awareness to Action

Vaping prevention in schools can easily become a one-way message. Students are given the facts and expected to make better choices. However, young people are also influenced by what they see online, what they hear from peers, and the messages they receive from family.

Deep Breaths takes a more active approach to vaping prevention for Year 10 students at Sunrise School.

Our Approach

Across a 10-hour learning journey, students move through five stages:

Understand→ Question→ Connect→ Co-design→ Create

Students begin by exploring what they already know about vaping and identifying common misconceptions. They then investigate misinformation, consider the social and digital influences around them, and connect their learning with family and community perspectives.

The issue

What Drives Vaping in Year 10?

Peers

Vaping is often a social activity, and vapes are commonly sourced through friends. Peer pressure is a common starting point.

Social Media and Marketing

Engaging with vaping content online is linked to more positive attitudes and lower perceived risk (Rutherford et al., 2023).

Stress and Coping

Older students may use vaping to relieve stress, anxiety or boredom. It is linked to weaker school and family connectedness (Donaldson et al., 2022).

Misconceptions

Many students believe vaping has no harmful effects, which increases their susceptibility (Pettigrew et al., 2023).

Health Impacts

Nicotine affects the adolescent brain while it is still developing, with lasting effects on attention and emotional regulation (Goriounova & Mansvelder, 2012). Vaping is also linked to asthma (Li et al., 2022), as well as to higher depression, anxiety and stress and lower academic self-efficacy (Novak et al., 2024).

Why Schools Must Respond

46% of Australian teachers see a student vaping at least once a month, yet only 51% say their school has clear vaping policies (Jongenelis & Robinson, 2023). School responses are often ad hoc, and students, parents and staff want age-appropriate curriculum and greater family and community involvement (Thomas et al., 2024).

Research and frameworks

Our Approach

What Guides the Project Design

The Year 10 vaping prevention project is guided by three connected approaches: Teaching for Wellbeing; Student Voice, Agency and Co-Design; and Family–School Partnerships. Together, these approaches move the project beyond simply telling students about the risks of vaping. Instead, students actively investigate the issue, contribute their perspectives, make decisions and apply their learning to a real health and wellbeing concern.

HIWS Framework

HIWS 6: Engage Students

The High Impact Wellbeing Strategies (HIWS) were developed by the Victorian Department of Education from evidence about teaching practices that can support student wellbeing (Allen et al., 2022). They are designed to help teachers embed wellbeing into everyday classroom practice and support a whole-school approach to wellbeing and Tier 1 prevention.

In the Deep Breaths project, this means students do more than receive information about vaping. They investigate misconceptions, question social media messages, explore the influences that shape young people's choices, and contribute ideas to the final prevention resource.

What Does Existing Research Tell Us?

Gardner et al., 2024

Knowledge & Intentions

A review by Gardner et al. (2024) found mixed evidence that school-based vaping programs reduce vaping over time. The programs were more effective in improving students' knowledge and attitudes around vaping.

This supports a focus on building students' knowledge and decision-making skills.

Read the Study ↗
Gardner et al., 2025

Student Feedback and Relevance

The Australian OurFutures Vaping program involved young people in developing the characters and storylines. Feedback from students and teachers during later testing led to changes in lesson length, storyline relevance and supporting resources.

This shows how student feedback can help make prevention education more relevant and appropriate for the young people it is designed for.

Read the Study ↗
Berti et al., 2023

Participation and Decision-Making

Student participation in whole-school initiatives can vary from students being passive recipients of information to taking more active roles in decision-making.

This supports giving students meaningful opportunities to investigate issues, contribute their perspectives and apply what they have learned.

Read the Study ↗
Livingston et al., 2022

Physical and Mental Health Effects

The review analysed 18 studies and found associations between vaping and respiratory problems, oral health problems and nicotine dependence. It also identified links between vaping, depression and suicidal ideation, although the authors note that much of the evidence is observational and cannot establish direct causation.

This paper shows why vaping should be treated as a health and wellbeing issue, rather than only a behavioural or disciplinary problem. It supports combining vaping prevention with mental health education and wellbeing support.

Read the Study ↗
Rutherford et al., 2023

Social Media Content Influence

Reviewing 32 studies involving more than 274,000 young people, the authors found that exposure to vaping-related social media content was associated with more positive attitudes towards vaping, lower perceptions of harm, and greater vaping use or susceptibility.

This supports teaching students about the commercial and social determinants of vaping. A school program should not only explain health risks, but also develop students' media literacy so they can critically examine influencers, advertising, flavours and online vaping content.

Read the Study ↗
Jongenelis & Robinson, 2023

Australian Secondary Schools and Educators

In a survey of 218 secondary school staff, 46% reported encountering a student with an e-cigarette at least monthly, while only around half reported having a specific school vaping policy. Teachers also described difficulties detecting vaping devices and a need for stronger educational resources. Punitive approaches such as suspension and expulsion were commonly reported.

This paper directly informs our whole-school approach. It demonstrates that inconsistent policies and punishment alone are insufficient, and supports clearer school policies, staff professional learning, prevention education and supportive intervention for students who vape.

Family–School Partnerships

Overview of Partnership

The Family–School Partnerships Framework provides a practical guide of suggested strategies for building and improving partnerships between schools and families. It is organised around seven key dimensions, each with its own set of strategies.

Communication

The communication dimension focuses on building constructive relationships through welcoming communication. Strategies include welcoming signage to promote inclusion, involving families in learning activities, involving families in setting and reviewing student goals throughout the year, and communicating information that helps families support learning and wellbeing.

The Framework ↗

Connecting Learning at Home and School

This dimension recognises that valuable learning can happen at home and in the community. Strategies include drawing on the interests of students and families outside school, providing videos or photos of classroom learning to families, and providing information to help home-based learning.

The Framework ↗

Recognise the Role of the Family

Parents are described as the primary educators of their children, with a lasting influence on attitude and achievement. Strategies include assessing whether current practices support family engagement, building family engagement into strategic plans, valuing families' knowledge of their child, and making school leaders visible.

Student Voice, Agency & Co-Design

Victorian policy positions students as participants in decisions that affect them. The Student Voice, Agency and Leadership policy states that students should have opportunities to share their experiences, give feedback and take part in decisions affecting themselves, their peers and their school community (Victorian Department of Education, 2026). FISO 2.0 includes activating student voice, agency and leadership within its Engagement core element (Department of Education and Training Victoria, 2021), and the Australian Student Wellbeing Framework names Student Voice as one of its five elements (Education Council, 2018).

In Deep Breaths, students decide which issues the campaign addresses and what its message, tone and format will be. See how student voice runs through the project →

How the Three Approaches Connect

Teaching for Wellbeing is how student voice happens in class. HIWS 6 asks teachers to offer choice and use students' feedback. Both are forms of student voice, so engaging students and giving them a say happen together.

Student voice makes partnerships two-way. Rather than the school simply sending information home, students lead conversations with trusted adults and bring those perspectives back into class. Families become contributors to students' thinking rather than recipients of information (Garbacz et al., 2020).

Partnerships give student voice a real purpose and audience. Family and community input shapes students' campaigns, and the final showcase gives their work an audience beyond the classroom. That real-world relevance is itself part of HIWS 6.

Together, the three approaches respond to the drivers of vaping identified in our brief: peer norms, family connectedness and school engagement.

HIWS 6 · Student voice

Teaching for Wellbeing

HIWS 6: Engage Students

High Impact Wellbeing Strategy 6: Engage Students supports learning that is relevant to students' lives and encourages active participation. Vaping is explored through situations students may encounter through peers, social media, family and the wider community.

Rather than relying on teacher-led information alone, students participate in collaborative inquiry, evaluate vaping information and misinformation, discuss different influences on health decisions and have choices in how they demonstrate their learning. This makes wellbeing part of the learning process rather than treating it as a separate topic.

Student Voice, Agency and Co-Design

Student voice is embedded throughout the project. Students are given opportunities to identify what they already know about vaping, recognise misconceptions and determine which issues are most relevant to Year 10 students.

Their involvement progresses through four stages:

ListenStudents contribute their perspectives through anonymous activities and hypothetical scenarios.
PrioritiseThey prioritise the three to five vaping issues they believe are most important.
Co-DesignIn groups, students decide the focus, message, tone and format of their prevention campaign.
Test and RefineFeedback from peers, families and wellbeing staff refines the campaign before it is shared with an authentic audience.

This creates genuine agency because students do more than choose between teacher-created options. Their ideas influence what is investigated, how information is communicated and what the final product looks like.

Keeping it safe

Because vaping is a sensitive issue, students are not required to disclose their own vaping behaviour. Anonymous polls and hypothetical scenarios allow students to contribute safely while still providing meaningful perspectives.

Learning Beyond the Classroom

Health decisions are influenced by more than what happens during lessons. Young people encounter vaping messages and attitudes through family, peers, social media and the wider community. The project therefore creates a two-way learning relationship between school and students' lives beyond the classroom.

Students take vaping-related questions and discussion prompts outside school and gather family or community perspectives. These ideas are then brought back into the classroom and considered alongside reliable health evidence.

The process can be represented as:

School→ Home→ Community→ Authentic Audience→ School

At school, students develop knowledge and critical thinking skills. At home, family members contribute their perspectives and experiences. Community and health contexts broaden students' understanding of vaping as a social and wellbeing issue. Students then use these different perspectives when making decisions about their final campaign.

This reflects the Family–School Partnerships principle of consulting families in decision-making rather than simply providing information to them. Families therefore become contributors to the learning process.

For Sunrise School, this approach is particularly valuable because the school community is culturally diverse. Consultation should therefore be inclusive and accessible so that different family and community perspectives can contribute to the project.

10 hours · three stages

The 10-Hour Journey

Building Knowledge

Hours 1–3 · Understand & Question

Across the first three hours, students move from what they think, to what they see, and then to what influences their views.

Hour 1

What Do We Know About Vaping?

Students begin with a short anonymous diagnostic poll exploring what they already know and believe about vaping, where they usually get their information from, and what questions they still have.

The responses help identify common misconceptions and give students some input into what the class explores next.

Key focus: prior knowledge, misconceptions, student voice

Hour 2

Sorting Facts From Misinformation

Students work collaboratively to investigate common vaping myths and social media claims. Using credible sources, they decide whether each claim is accurate and explain the evidence behind their judgement.

This activity develops students' ability to question vaping-related information.

Key focus: critical evaluation, media literacy, collaborative inquiry

Hour 3

What Shapes Your Views on Vaping?

Students then look more broadly at the influences that can shape young people's views of vaping, including peers, social media, advertising and family.

This activity extends beyond the classroom, encouraging students to compare different perspectives and consider how their views may be shaped by the people and information around them.

Key focus: social influences, self-awareness

Connect with Family and Community

Hours 4–6 · Connect

Across Hours 4–6, students engage in research and come to understand views in the community.

Hour 4

Conduct Research with Family and Community

Students develop a discussion prompt and bring it into the community. They are tasked with having a 5-minute discussion with two adults about what they think the impact of vaping is.

Any trusted adult is fine! A parent, mentor, sports coach or sibling is great, as long as they are an adult.

Key focus: critical evaluation, collaborative inquiry

Hour 5

Present Findings to a Family or Community Member

Students present their findings to one of the trusted adults they interviewed. They explain their findings and what they learned from discussing the issue with the two adults.

The trusted adult suggests what they would do to prevent vaping among students from a parent, family or community perspective.

Key focus: analysis

Hour 6
Formative assessment

Produce a Mini Report for the Teacher

Based on the findings from their discussions with adults, students develop a mini report to bring back into the classroom. This includes observations and prevention strategies suggested by family and community.

This activity brings students' findings from the community into the classroom.

Key focus: social influences, self-awareness

Co-Design and Create

Hours 7–10 · Co-design & Create

The final stage of the 10-hour learning journey gives students increasing responsibility for applying what they have learned. Students move from understanding vaping to creating an evidence-informed prevention message for their peers.

Hour 7

Listen to Students

Students complete a short anonymous poll and discuss hypothetical vaping scenarios involving situations such as peer pressure, stress, social media or school environments.

Rather than asking students to disclose personal vaping behaviour, the activity focuses on questions such as:

  • Why might young people choose to vape?
  • What pressures might Year 10 students experience?
  • What information about vaping is confusing?
  • What prevention messages would students actually pay attention to?
Hour 8

Co-Design the Campaign

Students work in groups to design their vaping prevention campaign. Each group decides:

  • the vaping issue they will focus on
  • the main prevention message
  • the intended audience
  • the tone of the campaign
  • the type of product they will create
  • the practical action or support message they want the audience to remember.

Possible products include a poster, short video, infographic or social media-style resource.

Groups consider evidence from earlier lessons together with student, family and wellbeing perspectives. This ensures that the final campaign is both evidence-informed and relevant to the school community.

Hours 9–10

Test, Improve and Share

Groups exchange their draft campaigns and conduct a peer review. Feedback considers whether the campaign:

  • contains accurate information
  • is understandable for the target audience
  • feels relevant to young people
  • uses a respectful and non-judgemental message
  • gives students a practical action or pathway for support.

Students decide which feedback to use and revise their campaign before the final presentation. See Learning to Action →

Learning to Action

Success Criteria

  • Students' final product demonstrates engagement with prior knowledge and shows reflection on what shapes students' views.
  • Students' final product shows engagement with the views of family and community.
  • Students' final product demonstrates the ability to communicate a message to the target audience through a campaign.
What students create

A vaping prevention campaign co-designed in small groups of 2–3.

It can be presented in a format of the students' choice, such as a poster, short video, infographic or social media post.

Who it's for

Middle school students, families, wellbeing staff and community partners.

What happens

Teams present their campaign and explain the decisions that informed it. The campaign should communicate a perspective based on personal convictions and the family and community beliefs explored throughout the learning journey.

The initial audience (the class) gives feedback on each campaign.

Example formats · not final products

What Could Students Create?

These are examples and templates only, to show what each format could look like. They are not finished campaigns. Each group creates its own campaign from scratch, choosing the format, message and design that best suit their chosen issue and audience.

Offering a range of formats reflects Universal Design for Learning (CAST, 2018). Each format presents information through multiple means of representation (visual, video, data and text-based), so campaign messages can reach audiences with different needs and preferences. Letting students choose how they create their campaign also gives them multiple means of action and expression to show what they have learned.

Example templates only. Students design their own content. Statistic in the poster and infographic examples is based on Scully et al. (2023): about 30% of Australian secondary students have tried vaping.

Evaluation

Measuring Impact

How We Will Know It Made a Difference

The project will be evaluated at three stages across the 10-hour learning journey. The aim is to identify changes in students' misconceptions, confidence in evaluating vaping information, and ability to make informed decisions.

1 · Before the project

Diagnostic Survey

Students complete a short anonymous survey at the beginning of the project. The survey establishes a baseline by exploring:

  • what students already know about vaping
  • common misconceptions
  • where students get their information
  • how confident they feel judging whether vaping information is reliable
  • how confident they feel making informed health decisions.

The results help identify what students already understand and what needs more attention during the project.

2 · During the project

Product Evaluation

Students' final vaping prevention resource will provide evidence of how well they can apply what they have learned. The resource can be evaluated using criteria such as:

  • accuracy of health information
  • use of credible evidence
  • ability to address misconceptions
  • consideration of different influences and perspectives
  • relevance to a Year 10 audience
  • clarity and usefulness of the prevention message.

This provides evidence of students' ability to move from simply knowing information to applying it in a meaningful context.

3 · After the project

Post-Survey

At the end of the 10-hour learning journey, students complete a post-survey using similar questions to the diagnostic survey. Comparing the two surveys can show changes in:

  • vaping-related knowledge
  • misconceptions
  • awareness of social and media influences
  • confidence in making informed decisions.

What Will Success Look Like?

Success will not be measured by whether students immediately stop vaping, or by whether long-term behaviour change can be demonstrated within the project.

Research suggests that school-based vaping programs show clearer short-term improvements in knowledge and intentions (Gardner et al., 2024). For this reason, the evaluation focuses on whether students leave the project better able to understand vaping, question the information they encounter and make informed health decisions.
